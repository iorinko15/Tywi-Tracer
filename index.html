    How the tracer works

    Pollution only travels downstream. If a reading is clean and the next reading downstream is high, something entered the river in between. The tracer turns that idea into numbers for every half-kilometre of river at once, and keeps updating as people log new readings.

    1. Put every reading on the river

    Each reading is snapped to the nearest point on the mapped river network (the Tywi plus the Brân, Sawdde, Cothi and Gwili) and given a distance from source in km. The mapped lines are simplified, so they are stretched by a winding factor of 1.25 to match real channel length.

    2. Work out how much water is flowing

    Drainage area grows downstream following Hack's law, plus each tributary's whole catchment where it joins:

    A(x) = c · x1.7 + Σ Atributaries above x

    Flow per km² of catchment depends on recent rain, using an antecedent precipitation index where yesterday's rain counts 80% as much as today's:

    API = Σd=0..13 raind · 0.8d    q = 0.012 + 0.004 · API    Q = A · q

    If a gauged flow at Nantgaredig is entered for that day, it replaces the rain estimate.

    3. Travel time and dilution

    Water speeds up as flow rises, v = 0.3 · Q0.25 m/s. Travel time from a possible entry point to a sample site is the integral of 1/v along the path, crossing confluences. A pollutant load L (g/s) entering upstream raises the concentration at a sample site by:

    ΔC = L · e−kτ / Q

    τ is travel time in days, k is natural loss (0.05/day for nitrate, 0.3/day for phosphate, which sticks to sediment and gets taken up by plants), and dividing by Q is dilution.

    4. Test every possible entry point

    For each half-km stretch of the network, and each possible start day, the tracer finds the load that best explains all readings by weighted least squares, then scores how much better that explains the data than "no extra source":

    score = Σ wi (yi − b)² / σi² − Σ wi (yi − b − ΔCi)² / σi²

    b is the background level (the lower 20% of recent readings unless set), σ is the expected error of a home test kit plus model error, and w halves the weight of a reading every half of the look-back window, so recent readings count more. Nitrate and phosphate are scored together, because slurry raises both.

    5. Turn scores into probabilities

    Scores become probabilities with Bayes' rule, P ∝ escore/2. The highlighted stretch holds 80% of the probability. If no stretch scores above 9 (roughly a 3-sigma signal) nothing is flagged. After finding the strongest source the tracer removes its effect and looks again, up to three sources.

    6. Where to test next

    The best next test site splits the likely stretch in half by probability. Whatever that reading shows, the search area roughly halves, like a binary search.

    Limits

    • River lines and catchment sizes are approximate; flow is estimated, not measured, unless a gauge reading is added.
    • Below Carmarthen the river is tidal, so those readings get three times the uncertainty.
    • Nitrate and phosphate alone can't tell slurry from sewage or fertiliser. Ammonia readings and known sewage overflow locations would help separate them.
    • A flagged stretch is a place to look, not proof. Confirm on the ground and report to Natural Resources Wales.
